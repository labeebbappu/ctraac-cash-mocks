What Approve does, in one transaction
  • Posts six voucher entries to Ahmed's cash book, dated today.
  • Moves his agreed balance −412.50 → −992.50.
  • Leaves line 2 on the submission as queried, not deleted — it comes back with the bill or it does not come back.
  • Closes SUB-0042 as approved. It is now a document, not a working list.
Sending it back releases the lines
A voucher belongs to at most one submission at a time. Send back unclaims every line, so Ahmed can correct them and submit again — the same shape as cancelling an invoice releasing its subscription slips. Skip the release and those vouchers are attached to a submission that no longer claims them: invisible to his next week, and never claimable again.
A queried line is answered by the staff member, never edited by the office. Correcting Ahmed's 12.50 into something with a bill behind it, on his behalf, produces a claim he did not make and a signature block that says he did.

One movement, two books, linked by counterpartEntryId. Reversing one reverses the other.
There is no separate “advance” type. Cash going to a staff member is one movement; whether it is a reimbursement or a float is just where the balance stood before it. Two types would mean deciding, for this 1,412.50, which 412.50 was which — a question with no answer and two screens to maintain.
Only approved amounts can be settled. SUB-0042 is greyed out above. Paying against an unapproved claim means the bills arrive after the cash, which is how a float and a reimbursement become impossible to tell apart.

Closing a period is the only thing that stops the book growing forever. Once signed, the period's entries are read-only and the next book opens with a single opening entry — which is why an approved entry is reversed rather than edited: a correction after signing has to be visible as its own line, dated after the signature.

What it costs to build

Two new tables on top of the cash-in-hand work, and six columns on the voucher. The ledger itself is not rebuilt.

A staff cash book is an adminCashAccount, not a second ledger
The cash-in-hand design already has accounts, entries, counterpart pairs and reversals, and custodianStaffId is already on the account. A staff book is that table with accountType = "staff". Building a parallel ledger would mean two sets of posting rules, and the handover between an office box and a staff member would have to be written twice — once in each, agreeing by hand.

Migration to the existing voucher
On top of the columns the cash-in-hand work adds, adminExpenseVouchers gains staffId, adminCashSubmissionId, claimStatus (draft · submitted · approved · queried · withdrawn), decidedAt and queryReason, all nullable. Every existing row reads as an office expense with no staff claim behind it, which is what they are. adminCashSubmissionId is the claim link and behaves exactly like appSubscriptionSlips.adminInvoiceId — claimed by a compare-and-swap, released when the submission is sent back.
The rules that have to hold
  • Balance is summed from entries, never stored on the account.
  • A posted entry is reversed, never edited or deleted.
  • A voucher belongs to at most one open submission; sending one back releases its lines.
  • Approval posts the entry on the approval's own transaction, with the audit row.
  • A settlement writes both entries or neither, linked by counterpartEntryId.
  • Only approved amounts are settleable; a pending claim is never payable.
  • Every claim line needs a receipt document, on the same gate the vendor-bill payment already uses.
  • A staff member with a login sees their own book and nobody else's; approving is a supervisor act.
The one thing this design cannot do, stated plainly. Because a staff book is never counted, it cannot detect a staff member spending a float on something they never claim — the balance simply sits there looking like cash they are holding. Ageing on days since last submission is the whole control for that case, which is why it is a column on the list screen rather than a detail buried in the book, and why Nasser's 24 days is as visible as Bilal's 18.
